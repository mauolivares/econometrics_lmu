1 Overview

This chapter revisits the empirical exercise from LaLonde (1986), whose goal is to evaluate a landmark training program in the United States: the National Supported Work Demonstration (NSW). The NSW was a temporary employment program designed to move disadvantaged workers, lacking basic job skills, into the labor market by providing subsidised work experience and counselling in a sheltered environment. Qualified applicants were assigned to training positions at random: the treatment group received the full NSW benefits, while the control group received none.

Our working sample consists of male participants with relatively poor labor market prospects. Because assignment was randomized, this data set is the natural laboratory in which to exercise the three approaches to inference developed in the course — asymptotic, randomization, and bootstrap — and to contrast the average treatment effect (ATE) with the quantile treatment effect (QTE).

2 Potential Outcomes and the Target Parameters

For individual \(i\) with binary treatment \(D_i \in \{0,1\}\) and pre-determined covariates \(X_i\), define the potential outcomes

\[ Y_i(1) = \text{earnings had } i \text{ participated}, \qquad Y_i(0) = \text{earnings had } i \text{ not participated}, \]

with observed earnings \(Y_i = D_i Y_i(1) + (1 - D_i) Y_i(0)\).

ImportantStable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA)

SUTVA requires (i) no interference — one unit’s potential outcomes do not depend on the treatment status of any other unit — and (ii) no hidden variation in treatment — there is a single, well-defined version of the program. SUTVA typically fails under spillovers (e.g. a trained worker displacing an untrained one from a job) or when the “treatment” bundles heterogeneous interventions.

We consider two target parameters. The average treatment effect

\[ \text{ATE} \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\big], \]

summarizes the program’s effect on the mean of earnings. The quantile treatment effect at level \(\tau \in (0,1)\),

\[ \text{QTE}_\tau \;=\; F_1^{-1}(\tau) - F_0^{-1}(\tau), \]

where \(F_d\) is the CDF of earnings in experimental arm \(d \in \{0,1\}\), describes the effect across the entire distribution of earnings. Neither the individual effect \(\beta_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\) nor these population parameters are directly observable, because for each \(i\) we see only one of the two potential outcomes.

2.1 Identification under Random Assignment

NoteRandom assignment restores identification

If \(\{(Y_i(1), Y_i(0))\}_{i=1}^n \perp \{D_i\}_{i=1}^n\), and \(0<P(D_i=1)<1\) for all \(i\), then the target parameters are expressed in terms of observables:

\[ \mathbb{E}[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)] = \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid D_i = 0], \]

\[ F_1^{-1}(\tau) - F_0^{-1}(\tau) = F^{-1}(\tau \mid D_i = 1) - F^{-1}(\tau \mid D_i = 0). \]

The obstacle to identification without exogenous randomization is selection bias. We studied two types of selection during the lecture. Selection on levels arises when treated and control units differ in their baseline outcome \(Y_i(0)\) (e.g. more motivated workers enrol and would have earned more anyway). Selection on gains arises when units select into treatment on the basis of their idiosyncratic return \(Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\). Random assignment cancels both channels, which is precisely why the NSW design is credible as a devide to uncover causal estimands, like ATEs or QTEs.

3 The Data

The experimental sample contains 445 male participants. The outcome is re78, real earnings in 1978 (post-treatment), in 1982 US dollars. Pre-treatment earnings (re74, re75), demographics, and schooling serve as covariates. Rather than the raw summary() output — which is hard to read and lists every dummy as a five-number summary — we report a compact balance table. Because assignment is randomized, the two arms should look alike on every pre-determined covariate, and Table 1 lets us check this at a glance: continuous variables are shown as the group mean (with the standard deviation in parentheses), and the many binary indicators as simple proportions.

4 Estimating the ATE

Under random assignment, the ATE is identified by the mean difference across experimental groups, and the principle of analogy tells us we can estimate it by the sample mean difference. Equivalently, it is the slope in a regression of earnings on a constant and the treatment indicator (why?).

Estimate the ATE two equivalent ways

# (ii) OLS slope

5 Asymptotic Inference

The four steps of inference are: (1) state the null, here \(H_0:\text{ATE}=0\); (2) choose a test statistic; (3) approximate its sampling distribution under \(H_0\); (4) apply a rejection rule. We use the studentized statistic

\[ T_n \;=\; \frac{\hat\beta - \beta_0}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta)} \;\overset{H_0}{=}\; \frac{\hat\beta}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta)}, \]

which is asymptotically standard normal under \(H_0\) because it is already studentized. Note that no explicit \(\sqrt{n}\) appears: the standard error \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta) = \sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\beta)/n}\) already carries the scaling.

The same statistic delivers all three equivalent rejection rules — a critical value, a \(p\)-value, and a confidence interval. The interval inverts the test: at level \(\alpha\) it collects the null values that \(T_n\) would not reject. By symmetry of the normal (\(z_{1-\alpha/2} = -z_{\alpha/2}\)), this is the familiar asymptotic interval

\[ \hat C_n(1-\alpha) = \Big[\,\hat\beta - z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta),\; \hat\beta + z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta)\,\Big], \]

which is exactly the object computed as asy_ci below.

ImportantA confidence interval is not a probability statement about the estimand

\(\hat C_n\) is random because it is built from data, and the guarantee is \(\mathbb{P}[\text{ATE} \in \hat C_n] \ge 1-\alpha\), at least asymptotically. Once the sample is realized there is no randomness left: a given realization \([L, U]\) either contains the ATE or it does not. The level \(1-\alpha\) describes the procedure’s coverage — “confidence” — not a probability for this particular interval.

Studentized test statistic as a function of (Y, D) 
t_stat <- function(outcome, treatment) {
  mod         <- lm(outcome ~ 1 + treatment)
  se_beta_hat <- summary(mod)$coefficients[2, 2]   # SE from THIS fit
  tn          <- mod$coefficients[2] / se_beta_hat
  unname(tn)
}

6 Randomization Inference

ImportantRandomization Hypothesis

Let \(\mathbf{G}_n\) be a group of transformations acting on the data \(W^{(n)}\). The law \(P_n\) satisfies the randomization hypothesis if \(W^{(n)} \stackrel{d}{=} g\,W^{(n)}\) for all \(g \in \mathbf{G}_n\) whenever \(P_n \in \Omega_0\). When it holds, the permutation test is exact in finite samples for any test statistic.

A subtlety arises here. The null \(H_0:\text{ATE}=0\) is a statement about means, not about the joint distribution, so the randomization hypothesis does not hold for it in general: two distributions can share the same mean while differing elsewhere (e.g., different variances), and permuting labels would then change the distribution of the data. Nonetheless, when testing equality of means with a properly studentized statistic, we showed during lecture that the permutation test controls size asymptotically (Chung and Romano 2013). While the asymptotic statements are only theoretical devices, one could argue that for \(n = 445\), the approximation might be adequate. We can inspect this claim empirically too.

Build the randomization distribution of T_n

TipThe sharp null makes the test exact

Consider instead Fisher’s sharp null \(H_0: Y_i(1) = Y_i(0)\) for all \(i\) (almost surely or in distribution). Under an i.i.d. sample this null implies that the vector of outcomes is invariant in distribution to any permutation of the treatment labels, so the randomization hypothesis holds exactly. The same construction of the permutation distribution then yields a test that is valid in finite samples, not only asymptotically. Because \(\mathbf{G}_n\) has \(n!\) elements, we sidestep exact over all permutations by drawing \(B \ll n!\) permutations uniformly at random, as above.

7 Bootstrap Inference

The empirical bootstrap is the plug-in estimator of the sampling distribution: it replaces the unknown law \(P\) by the empirical measure \(\hat P_n\) and reads off the distribution of \(T_n\) under \(\hat P_n\). Exact evaluation requires \(n^n\) resamples, so in practice we use the Monte Carlo approximation. That is, we draw \(B\) resamples with replacement and recompute the recentered, studentized statistic on each:

\[ T_n^\star \;=\; \frac{\hat\beta^\star - \hat\beta}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta^\star)}. \]

Recentering at \(\hat\beta\) (rather than \(\beta_0\)) is what makes \(T_n^\star\) mimic the distribution of \(T_n\) under \(H_0\).

Build the bootstrap distribution of T_n 
bootstrap_tn <- function(data = lalonde.exp, n_boot = 1000) {
  tn_star <- rep(NA, n_boot)
  theta_n <- lm(re78 ~ treat, data = data)$coefficients[2]
  for (i in 1:n_boot) {
    boot_data  <- data[sample(nrow(data), replace = TRUE), ]
    boot_model <- lm(re78 ~ treat, data = boot_data)
    tn_star[i] <- (boot_model$coefficients[2] - theta_n) /
                   summary(boot_model)$coefficients[2, 2]
  }
  tn_star
}

tn_star <- bootstrap_tn(data = lalonde.exp, n_boot = n_boot)
tn_star <- c(tn_star, Tn_obs)   # append the observed statistic

7.1 Confidence Intervals from the Bootstrap

The equivalence between tests and intervals carries over to the bootstrap: we only replace the normal quantile \(z_{1-\alpha/2}\) by a quantile of the bootstrap law of \(T_n^\star\). Write \(J_n(\cdot,\hat P_n)\) for that law and \(J_n^{-1}(\tau,\hat P_n) = \inf\{t : J_n(t,\hat P_n) \ge \tau\}\) for its quantiles; the upper quantile \(J_n^{-1}(1-\alpha/2,\hat P_n)\) is precisely the critical value cn_boot_alpha computed above.

The percentile bootstrap interval takes the infeasible interval \(\{\theta : J_n^{-1}(\alpha/2,P) \le T_n \le J_n^{-1}(1-\alpha/2,P)\}\) and replaces the unknown \(P\) by \(\hat P_n\). For the studentized statistic \(T_n^\star\) this delivers the percentile-\(t\) interval

\[ \hat C_n^B(1-\alpha) = \Big[\,\hat\beta - J_n^{-1}(1-\tfrac{\alpha}{2},\hat P_n)\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta),\; \hat\beta - J_n^{-1}(\tfrac{\alpha}{2},\hat P_n)\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta)\,\Big]. \]

Note the quantile reversal: the upper bootstrap quantile enters the lower endpoint. Under bootstrap consistency, \(\mathbb{P}[\text{ATE} \in \hat C_n^B(1-\alpha)] \to 1-\alpha\), and — exactly as with the asymptotic interval — we reject \(H_0:\text{ATE}=0\) whenever \(0 \notin \hat C_n^B(1-\alpha)\).

8 Quantile Treatment Effects

The ATE conceals distributional heterogeneity: a program can lift average earnings while leaving the bottom of the distribution untouched. The QTE fills this gap. Under random assignment the marginal CDFs \(F_1, F_0\) are identified from the two experimental groups, so a first estimate is simply the difference of empirical quantiles.

Empirical quantile differences

8.1 Interpreting the QTE

How we read these numbers depends on an assumption about how individuals are ranked in the two potential-outcome distributions.

NoteCase I — Rank invariance

Under rank invariance, an individual occupies the same rank in \(F_0\) and \(F_1\): the person at the \(\tau\)-quantile of the control distribution is the same person who would be at the \(\tau\)-quantile of the treated distribution. Then \(\text{QTE}_\tau\) is the treatment effect for that individual. For example, a QTE of \(0\) at \(\tau = 0.2\), and of about \(1{,}124\) at \(\tau = 0.5\), and of about \(2{,}278\) at \(\tau = 0.8\) would say the program had no effect for low earners, a moderate effect at the median, and a larger effect for higher earners. Under rank invariance, each statement is attached to specific people.

WarningCase II — Rank reversals

Rank invariance is strong and usually unrealistic. If the NSW program induces rank reversals — reshuffling who ends up where in the earnings distribution — then the difference of medians (or of any pair of quantiles) no longer identifies the effect for a given person. Knowing the median gap tells us nothing about the treatment effect of the individual who would have been at the median absent treatment. In this (more realistic) case the QTE is interpreted purely as a distributional statement: it measures the shift in the quantiles of the earnings distribution caused by the program, not an individual-level effect.

8.2 QTE with Covariates

When identification relies on covariates rather than pure randomization, the QTE requires a conditional independence assumption. The qte package accommodates covariates through the xformla argument; we again report both asymptotic and bootstrap inference, using two pre-determined covariates.

```{r}
qte_frame <- function(obj) {
  tau   <- if (!is.null(obj$probs)) obj$probs else seq(0.05, 0.95, 0.05)
  se    <- obj$qte.se
  lower <- if (!is.null(obj$qte.lower)) obj$qte.lower else obj$qte - qnorm(0.975) * se
  upper <- if (!is.null(obj$qte.upper)) obj$qte.upper else obj$qte + qnorm(0.975) * se
  data.frame(tau = tau, qte = obj$qte, lower = lower, upper = upper)
}
```

# Overview {#sec-overview}

This chapter revisits the empirical exercise from @lalonde1986, whose goal is to
evaluate a landmark training program in the United States: the **National
Supported Work Demonstration (NSW)**. The NSW was a temporary employment program
designed to move disadvantaged workers, lacking basic job skills, into the labor
market by providing subsidised work experience and counselling in a sheltered
environment. Qualified applicants were assigned to training positions **at
random**: the treatment group received the full NSW benefits, while the control
group received none.

Our working sample consists of male participants with relatively poor labor
market prospects. Because assignment was randomized, this data set is the natural
laboratory in which to exercise the three approaches to inference developed in
the course — **asymptotic**, **randomization**, and **bootstrap** — and to
contrast the **average treatment effect (ATE)** with the **quantile treatment
effect (QTE)**.

# Potential Outcomes and the Target Parameters {#sec-po}

For individual $i$ with binary treatment $D_i \in \{0,1\}$ and pre-determined
covariates $X_i$, define the potential outcomes

$$
Y_i(1) = \text{earnings had } i \text{ participated}, \qquad
Y_i(0) = \text{earnings had } i \text{ not participated},
$$

with observed earnings $Y_i = D_i Y_i(1) + (1 - D_i) Y_i(0)$.

::: {.callout-important}
## Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA)
SUTVA requires (i) **no interference** — one unit's potential outcomes do not
depend on the treatment status of any other unit — and (ii) **no hidden
variation in treatment** — there is a single, well-defined version of the
program. SUTVA typically fails under spillovers (e.g. a trained worker displacing
an untrained one from a job) or when the "treatment" bundles heterogeneous
interventions.
:::

We consider two target parameters. The **average treatment effect**

$$
\text{ATE} \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\big],
$$

summarizes the program's effect on the *mean* of earnings. The **quantile
treatment effect** at level $\tau \in (0,1)$,

$$
\text{QTE}_\tau \;=\; F_1^{-1}(\tau) - F_0^{-1}(\tau),
$$

where $F_d$ is the CDF of earnings in experimental arm $d \in \{0,1\}$, describes
the effect across the *entire distribution* of earnings. Neither the individual
effect $\beta_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)$ nor these population parameters are directly
observable, because for each $i$ we see only one of the two potential outcomes.

## Identification under Random Assignment {#sec-identification}

::: {.callout-note}
## Random assignment restores identification
If $\{(Y_i(1), Y_i(0))\}_{i=1}^n \perp \{D_i\}_{i=1}^n$, and $0<P(D_i=1)<1$ for all $i$, then the target
parameters are expressed in terms of observables:

$$
\mathbb{E}[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)] = \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid D_i = 0],
$$

$$
F_1^{-1}(\tau) - F_0^{-1}(\tau) = F^{-1}(\tau \mid D_i = 1) - F^{-1}(\tau \mid D_i = 0).
$$
:::

The obstacle to identification without exogenous randomization is **selection bias**.
We studied two types of selection during the lecture. *Selection on levels* arises when treated 
and control units differ in their baseline outcome $Y_i(0)$ (e.g. more motivated workers enrol *and* would have
earned more anyway). *Selection on gains* arises when units select into treatment
on the basis of their idiosyncratic return $Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)$. Random assignment
cancels both channels, which is precisely why the NSW design is credible as a devide
to uncover causal estimands, like ATEs or QTEs.

# The Data {#sec-data}

The experimental sample contains `r nrow(lalonde.exp)` male participants. The

Pre-treatment earnings (`re74`, `re75`), demographics, and schooling serve as
covariates. Rather than the raw `summary()` output — which is hard to read and

# Estimating the ATE {#sec-ate}

Under random assignment, the ATE is identified by the mean difference across experimental
groups, and the principle of analogy tells us we can estimate it by the *sample* mean difference.
Equivalently, it is the slope in a regression of earnings on a constant and the treatment indicator (why?).

```{r}
#| label: ate-estimate
#| code-summary: "Estimate the ATE two equivalent ways"

# (i) Sample mean difference
ate_1 <- mean(lalonde.exp$re78[lalonde.exp$treat == 1]) -
         mean(lalonde.exp$re78[lalonde.exp$treat == 0])

# (ii) OLS slope
mod1 <- lm(re78 ~ treat, data = lalonde.exp)

c(mean_difference = ate_1, ols_slope = unname(mod1$coefficients[2]))
```

The two estimates coincide: the estimated ATE is roughly
`r round(ate_1, 2)` dollars.

# Asymptotic Inference {#sec-asymptotic}

The four steps of inference are: (1) state the null, here $H_0:\text{ATE}=0$;
(2) choose a test statistic; (3) approximate its sampling distribution under
$H_0$; (4) apply a rejection rule. We use the studentized statistic

$$
T_n \;=\; \frac{\hat\beta - \beta_0}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta)}
\;\overset{H_0}{=}\; \frac{\hat\beta}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta)},
$$

which is asymptotically standard normal under $H_0$ because it is already
studentized. Note that no explicit $\sqrt{n}$ appears: the standard error
$\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta) = \sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\beta)/n}$
already carries the scaling.

The same statistic delivers all three equivalent rejection rules — a critical
value, a $p$-value, and a confidence interval. The interval *inverts* the test:
at level $\alpha$ it collects the null values that $T_n$ would not reject. By
symmetry of the normal ($z_{1-\alpha/2} = -z_{\alpha/2}$), this is the familiar
asymptotic interval

$$
\hat C_n(1-\alpha) = \Big[\,\hat\beta - z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta),\;
                          \hat\beta + z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta)\,\Big],
$$

which is exactly the object computed as `asy_ci` below.

::: {.callout-important}
## A confidence interval is not a probability statement about the estimand
$\hat C_n$ is random because it is built from data, and the guarantee is
$\mathbb{P}[\text{ATE} \in \hat C_n] \ge 1-\alpha$, at least asymptotically. Once the
sample is realized there is no randomness left: a given realization $[L, U]$ either
contains the ATE or it does not. The level $1-\alpha$ describes the *procedure's*
coverage — "confidence" — not a probability for this particular interval.
:::


```{r}
#| label: t-stat-fn
#| code-summary: "Studentized test statistic as a function of (Y, D)"

t_stat <- function(outcome, treatment) {
  mod         <- lm(outcome ~ 1 + treatment)
  se_beta_hat <- summary(mod)$coefficients[2, 2]   # SE from THIS fit
  tn          <- mod$coefficients[2] / se_beta_hat
  unname(tn)
}
```

```{r}
#| label: asymptotic-test
#| code-summary: "Asymptotic test, p-value, and confidence interval"

Tn_obs <- t_stat(lalonde.exp$re78, lalonde.exp$treat)

# Rejection rule: reject if |T_n| exceeds the normal critical value
reject_asy <- (Tn_obs > qnorm(1 - alpha / 2))

# p-value straight from the regression table
p_value <- summary(mod1)$coefficients[2, 4]

# 95% asymptotic CI: beta_hat +/- z_{1-alpha} * SE
se_beta_hat <- summary(mod1)$coefficients[2, 2]
asy_ci <- mod1$coefficients[2] + c(-1, 1) * qnorm(1 - alpha / 2) * se_beta_hat

cat("T_n           :", round(Tn_obs, 4), "\n")
cat("Reject H0     :", reject_asy, "\n")
cat("p-value       :", round(p_value, 4), "\n")
cat("95% CI (ATE)  : [", round(asy_ci[1], 2), ",", round(asy_ci[2], 2), "]\n")
```

The estimated ATE is significant at the 5% level, and the confidence interval
excludes zero: the NSW program raises male earnings by about
`r round(ate_1, 0)` dollars per year on average (in 1982 dollars).

# Randomization Inference {#sec-randomization}

::: {.callout-important}
## Randomization Hypothesis
Let $\mathbf{G}_n$ be a group of transformations acting on the data $W^{(n)}$.
The law $P_n$ satisfies the randomization hypothesis if
$W^{(n)} \stackrel{d}{=} g\,W^{(n)}$ for all $g \in \mathbf{G}_n$ whenever
$P_n \in \Omega_0$. When it holds, the permutation test is **exact in finite
samples** for any test statistic.
:::

A subtlety arises here. The null $H_0:\text{ATE}=0$ is a statement about *means*,
not about the *joint distribution*, so the randomization hypothesis does **not**
hold for it in general: two distributions can share the same mean while differing
elsewhere (e.g., different variances), and permuting labels would then change the distribution of the data.
Nonetheless, when testing equality of means with a **properly studentized**
statistic, we showed during lecture that the permutation test controls size *asymptotically* [@chungromano2013]. 
While the asymptotic statements are only theoretical devices, one could argue that for
$n = 445$, the approximation might be adequate. We can inspect this claim empirically too.

```{r}
#| label: permutation-dist
#| code-summary: "Build the randomization distribution of T_n"

data_subset <- lalonde.exp[, c("re78", "treat")]

# Permute the treatment vector n_perm times ...
sample_ind     <- lapply(1:n_perm, function(x) sample(seq_along(data_subset$treat)))
perm_data_list <- lapply(sample_ind, function(x, db) db[x], data_subset$treat)
perm_data      <- do.call(cbind, perm_data_list)

# ... and append the original assignment as the last column
perm_data <- cbind(perm_data, data_subset$treat)

# Recompute T_n under each (permuted) assignment
t_stat_perm <- apply(perm_data, 2, function(x) t_stat(data_subset[, 1], x))

# Permutation critical value and rejection decision
cn_alpha <- quantile(t_stat_perm, 1 - alpha / 2)
c(perm_cv = unname(cn_alpha), normal_cv = qnorm(1 - alpha / 2))
(Tn_obs > cn_alpha)
```

The permutation critical value settles around the the quantile of the
standard normal distribution $z_{0.975}$ as the sample size grows. Indeed,
the two tests reach the same conclusion: we reject $H_0:\text{ATE}=0$.

```{r}
#| label: fig-rand-dist
#| fig-cap: "Randomization distribution of $T_n$ over 1000 permutations, with the permutation and asymptotic (normal) critical values."
#| code-summary: "Plot the randomization distribution"

cv_rand <- data.frame(
  value     = c(qnorm(1 - alpha / 2), cn_alpha),
  Reference = factor(c("Asymptotic (normal)", "Permutation"),
                     levels = c("Asymptotic (normal)", "Permutation"))
)

ggplot(data.frame(t_stat_perm = t_stat_perm), aes(x = t_stat_perm)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 30, fill = "grey85", color = "white") +
  geom_vline(data = cv_rand, aes(xintercept = value, color = Reference),
             linetype = "dashed", linewidth = 1) +
  scale_color_manual(values = c("Asymptotic (normal)" = "grey30",
                                "Permutation"         = col_control),
                     name = NULL) +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 13) +
  theme(legend.position = "top") +
  labs(title = "Randomization Distribution of the Test Statistic",
       x = expression(T[n]), y = "Count")
```

::: {.callout-tip}
## The sharp null makes the test *exact*
Consider instead Fisher's **sharp null** $H_0: Y_i(1) = Y_i(0)$ for all $i$ 
(almost surely or in distribution). Under an i.i.d. sample this null implies 
that the vector of outcomes is invariant in distribution to any permutation 
of the treatment labels, so the randomization hypothesis holds *exactly*. 
The same construction of the permutation distribution then yields a test that 
is valid in finite samples, not only asymptotically. Because $\mathbf{G}_n$ has $n!$ 
elements, we sidestep exact over all permutations by drawing $B \ll n!$ permutations 
uniformly at random, as above.
:::

# Bootstrap Inference {#sec-bootstrap}

The empirical bootstrap is the **plug-in** estimator of the sampling
distribution: it replaces the unknown law $P$ by the empirical measure
$\hat P_n$ and reads off the distribution of $T_n$ under $\hat P_n$. Exact
evaluation requires $n^n$ resamples, so in practice we use the **Monte Carlo
approximation**. That is, we draw $B$ resamples with replacement and recompute the
recentered, studentized statistic on each:

$$
T_n^\star \;=\; \frac{\hat\beta^\star - \hat\beta}{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta^\star)}.
$$

Recentering at $\hat\beta$ (rather than $\beta_0$) is what makes $T_n^\star$
mimic the distribution of $T_n$ under $H_0$.

```{r}
#| label: bootstrap-dist
#| code-summary: "Build the bootstrap distribution of T_n"

bootstrap_tn <- function(data = lalonde.exp, n_boot = 1000) {
  tn_star <- rep(NA, n_boot)
  theta_n <- lm(re78 ~ treat, data = data)$coefficients[2]
  for (i in 1:n_boot) {
    boot_data  <- data[sample(nrow(data), replace = TRUE), ]
    boot_model <- lm(re78 ~ treat, data = boot_data)
    tn_star[i] <- (boot_model$coefficients[2] - theta_n) /
                   summary(boot_model)$coefficients[2, 2]
  }
  tn_star
}

tn_star <- bootstrap_tn(data = lalonde.exp, n_boot = n_boot)
tn_star <- c(tn_star, Tn_obs)   # append the observed statistic

cn_boot_alpha <- quantile(tn_star, 1 - alpha / 2)
c(boot_cv = unname(cn_boot_alpha),
  perm_cv = unname(cn_alpha),
  normal_cv = qnorm(1 - alpha / 2))
(Tn_obs > cn_boot_alpha)
```

Observe that indeed, all three critical values are close, and all three tests reject
$H_0:\text{ATE}=0$.

```{r}
#| label: fig-boot-dist
#| fig-cap: "Bootstrap distribution of $T_n$, with the asymptotic, permutation, and bootstrap critical values."
#| code-summary: "Plot the bootstrap distribution"
```

## Confidence Intervals from the Bootstrap {#sec-boot-ci}

The equivalence between tests and intervals carries over to the bootstrap: we
only replace the normal quantile $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ by a quantile of the bootstrap
law of $T_n^\star$. Write $J_n(\cdot,\hat P_n)$ for that law and
$J_n^{-1}(\tau,\hat P_n) = \inf\{t : J_n(t,\hat P_n) \ge \tau\}$ for its
quantiles; the upper quantile $J_n^{-1}(1-\alpha/2,\hat P_n)$ is precisely the
critical value `cn_boot_alpha` computed above.

The **percentile bootstrap interval** takes the infeasible interval
$\{\theta : J_n^{-1}(\alpha/2,P) \le T_n \le J_n^{-1}(1-\alpha/2,P)\}$ and
replaces the unknown $P$ by $\hat P_n$. For the studentized statistic
$T_n^\star$ this delivers the **percentile-$t$** interval

$$
\hat C_n^B(1-\alpha) = \Big[\,\hat\beta - J_n^{-1}(1-\tfrac{\alpha}{2},\hat P_n)\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta),\;
                            \hat\beta - J_n^{-1}(\tfrac{\alpha}{2},\hat P_n)\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta)\,\Big].
$$

Note the **quantile reversal**: the *upper* bootstrap quantile enters the *lower*
endpoint. Under bootstrap consistency,
$\mathbb{P}[\text{ATE} \in \hat C_n^B(1-\alpha)] \to 1-\alpha$, and — exactly as with
the asymptotic interval — we reject $H_0:\text{ATE}=0$ whenever
$0 \notin \hat C_n^B(1-\alpha)$.

```{r}
#| label: bootstrap-ci
#| code-summary: "Studentized (percentile-t) bootstrap CI for the ATE"

q_lo <- quantile(tn_star, alpha / 2)       # J_n^{-1}(alpha/2,   Phat)
q_hi <- quantile(tn_star, 1 - alpha / 2)   # J_n^{-1}(1-alpha/2, Phat)

boot_ci <- c(lower = unname(mod1$coefficients[2] - q_hi * se_beta_hat),
             upper = unname(mod1$coefficients[2] - q_lo * se_beta_hat))

round(boot_ci, 2)
(boot_ci["lower"] > 0 | boot_ci["upper"] < 0)   # TRUE => reject H0: ATE = 0
```

The interval excludes zero, agreeing with the asymptotic and permutation
conclusions. The confidence bands drawn on the QTE figures below are the
analogous bootstrap intervals, computed pointwise at each quantile $\tau$. The
Efron ordering and the standard-error plug-in variants (Case I and Case II) are
developed in the dedicated bootstrap confidence-interval chapter.

# Quantile Treatment Effects {#sec-qte}

The ATE conceals distributional heterogeneity: a program can lift average
earnings while leaving the bottom of the distribution untouched. The QTE fills
this gap. Under random assignment the marginal CDFs $F_1, F_0$ are identified
from the two experimental groups, so a first estimate is simply the difference 
of empirical quantiles.

```{r}
#| label: qte-manual
#| code-summary: "Empirical quantile differences"

quantiles          <- seq(0.10, 0.90, by = 0.05)
control_quantiles  <- quantile(lalonde.exp$re78[lalonde.exp$treat == 0], probs = quantiles)
treatment_quantiles<- quantile(lalonde.exp$re78[lalonde.exp$treat == 1], probs = quantiles)

qte_sample <- treatment_quantiles - control_quantiles
round(qte_sample, 2)
```

The `qte` package performs this calculations, and supplies standard errors,
by default via the asymptotic approximation, and also via the bootstrap
(we need only specify the number of Monte Carlo draws; in this case, 1000 bootstrap
samples). The function `ci.qtet` targets the quantile treatment effect on the treated 
(QTT), which coincides with the QTE under random assignment.

```{r}
#| label: qte-package

#| warning: false
```

## Interpreting the QTE {#sec-qte-interpretation}

How we read these numbers depends on an assumption about how individuals are
*ranked* in the two potential-outcome distributions.

::: {.callout-note}
## Case I — Rank invariance
Under **rank invariance**, an individual occupies the same rank in $F_0$ and
$F_1$: the person at the $\tau$-quantile of the control distribution is the same
person who would be at the $\tau$-quantile of the treated distribution. Then
$\text{QTE}_\tau$ *is* the treatment effect for that individual. For example, a
QTE of $0$ at $\tau = 0.2$, and of about $1{,}124$ at $\tau = 0.5$, 
and of about $2{,}278$ at $\tau = 0.8$ would say the program had no effect for low earners, a
moderate effect at the median, and a larger effect for higher earners. Under rank invariance, 
each statement is attached to specific people.
:::

::: {.callout-warning}
## Case II — Rank reversals
Rank invariance is strong and usually unrealistic. If the NSW program induces
**rank reversals** — reshuffling who ends up where in the earnings distribution —
then the difference of medians (or of any pair of quantiles) no longer identifies
the effect *for a given person*. Knowing the median gap tells us nothing about
the treatment effect of the individual who would have been at the median absent
treatment. In this (more realistic) case the QTE is interpreted purely as a
**distributional statement**: it measures the shift in the quantiles of the
earnings distribution caused by the program, not an individual-level effect.
:::
