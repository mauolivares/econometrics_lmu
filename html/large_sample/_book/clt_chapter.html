4  Central Limit Theorem

The Law of Large Numbers tells us that \(\hat{\beta}\) converges to \(\beta\) as \(n\) grows. The Central Limit Theorem goes further: it tells us the rate at which this happens and, more strikingly, the shape of the sampling distribution.

Specifically, under standard regularity conditions and an i.i.d. sample with finite variance:

\[\sqrt{n}\!\left(\mathbb{E}_n[Y] - \mathbb{E}[Y]\right) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\, \mathbb{V}[Y]\right)\]

The key implication: if we recenter the sample mean by subtracting the true population mean and rescale by \(\sqrt{n}\), the resulting quantity converges in distribution to a standard normal — no matter what the underlying distribution of \(Y\) is, provided it has finite variance.

ImportantWhy this is remarkable

The normality in the limit does not come from \(Y\) being normally distributed. It emerges purely from the averaging process itself. This is the mathematical foundation for \(t\)-tests and confidence intervals in virtually all of econometrics.

4.1 The scaled and centered sum

Let \(Y_1, \ldots, Y_n\) be i.i.d. with mean \(\mu = \mathbb{E}[Y]\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2 = \mathbb{V}[Y]\). Define the sample mean:

\[\mathbb{E}_n[Y] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i\]

Now consider the centered version, scaled by \(\sqrt{n}\):

\[S_n \;=\; \sqrt{n}\!\left(\mathbb{E}_n[Y] - \mu\right) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^n (Y_i - \mu)\]

Step 1 — Mean is zero.

\[\mathbb{E}[S_n] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[Y_i - \mu] = 0\]

Step 2 — Variance is \(\sigma^2\).

By independence:

\[\mathbb{V}[S_n] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{V}[Y_i - \mu] = \frac{1}{n} \cdot n\sigma^2 = \sigma^2\]

NoteWhy multiply by \(\sqrt{n}\) and not \(n\)?

The raw sample mean \(\mathbb{E}_n[Y]\) has variance \(\sigma^2/n\), which collapses to zero. Multiplying by \(\sqrt{n}\) exactly offsets this shrinkage, keeping the variance at a fixed, finite level \(\sigma^2\). This is why \(\sqrt{n}\) is the right normalizing rate — it is the unique rate that produces a non-degenerate limit.

4.2 Why the normal? The characteristic function argument

To see why the limit is normal and not something else, consider the characteristic function (Fourier transform) of \(S_n\). Let \(\phi_Y(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{itY}]\) be the characteristic function of a single centered observation \(Y_i - \mu\).

Since the \(Y_i\) are i.i.d., the characteristic function of \(S_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum(Y_i - \mu)\) is:

\[\phi_{S_n}(t) = \left[\phi_Y\!\left(\tfrac{t}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right]^n\]

Now expand \(\phi_Y\) in a Taylor series around \(t = 0\). Using \(\mathbb{E}[Y_i - \mu] = 0\) and \(\mathbb{E}[(Y_i-\mu)^2] = \sigma^2\):

\[\phi_Y\!\left(\tfrac{t}{\sqrt{n}}\right) = 1 - \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2n} - \underbrace{\frac{i^3 \kappa_3}{6} \cdot \frac{t^3}{n^{3/2}}}_{\text{skewness term}} - \underbrace{\frac{\kappa_4}{24} \cdot \frac{t^4}{n^2}}_{\text{kurtosis term}} - \cdots\]

where \(\kappa_3, \kappa_4, \ldots\) are the cumulants (skewness, excess kurtosis, …) of \(Y\).

Raising this to the \(n\)-th power and taking \(n \to \infty\):

\[\phi_{S_n}(t) = \left[1 - \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2n} - O(n^{-3/2})\right]^n \;\longrightarrow\; e^{-\sigma^2 t^2/2}\]

This is precisely the characteristic function of \(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\).

ImportantThe key insight

Notice what happens to each higher-order term as \(n \to \infty\):

Term Shrinks at rate
Variance (\(\kappa_2\)) Stays fixed (by design of the \(\sqrt{n}\) scaling)
Skewness (\(\kappa_3\)) \(n^{-1/2} \to 0\)
Excess kurtosis (\(\kappa_4\)) \(n^{-1} \to 0\)
\(k\)-th cumulant (\(\kappa_k\), \(k \geq 3\)) \(n^{-(k-2)/2} \to 0\)

Averaging destroys higher-moment information faster than it destroys variance. The mean and variance are pinned — everything else vanishes. The normal distribution is the unique distribution with finite variance for which all cumulants beyond the second are zero. It is the only distribution that survives.

4.3 Three angles of intuition

Students often accept the statement of the CLT but struggle to see why the normal appears rather than some other distribution. Here are three complementary perspectives.

4.3.1 Angle 1: The normal as a fixed point

The characteristic function argument above shows that repeated averaging is an operator on distributions, and \(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\) is the fixed point of that operator. Any distribution with finite variance gets pulled toward the normal under averaging, the way iterating a contraction mapping pulls you to a fixed point.

4.3.2 Angle 2: Entropy maximization

Among all distributions with a fixed mean \(\mu\) and fixed variance \(\sigma^2\), the normal distribution uniquely maximizes entropy (differential Shannon entropy). Entropy measures how much information — or “interesting structure” — a distribution carries beyond its first two moments.

When you average \(n\) i.i.d. draws, the skewness of the result shrinks at rate \(1/\sqrt{n}\), the excess kurtosis at rate \(1/n\), and so on. All the idiosyncratic structure of the original distribution is progressively erased. What remains, once only the mean and variance are left standing, is the distribution that makes the fewest additional claims — the one with maximum entropy. That distribution is the normal.

An analogy that often lands. Imagine describing a person’s face with many numbers: eye color, nose shape, jawline, freckles, skin texture. Now suppose a process keeps blurring the photo slightly at each step. The specific details — freckles, eye color — disappear first. Eventually all you can make out is the rough oval of a face. That oval is the “maximum entropy face” given the constraint that it’s still a human head. The normal distribution is the statistical equivalent of that oval.

4.3.3 Angle 3: Convolutions smooth everything out

When you add two independent random variables, you convolve their densities. Each convolution makes the result smoother and more symmetric — it irons out bumps, fills in gaps, and spreads mass more evenly. After enough convolutions, the shape is entirely determined by just the mean and variance, and the only shape with those two properties and no others is the normal.

Students can see this happening in the simulation below: even a bimodal or heavily skewed population produces a beautifully bell-shaped sampling distribution once \(n\) is large enough.

4.5 Key takeaways

  1. Centering and scaling matter. The raw sample mean collapses to a point (LLN). Multiplying by \(\sqrt{n}\) holds the variance fixed and reveals the distributional shape.

  2. Higher moments vanish at increasing rates. Skewness shrinks at \(n^{-1/2}\), kurtosis at \(n^{-1}\), and so on. The \(\sqrt{n}\)-scaled sum retains only the variance.

  3. The normal is the attractor. It is the unique fixed point of the averaging operator for distributions with finite variance, the maximum-entropy distribution given mean and variance, and the limit of any convolution sequence. It is not assumed — it emerges.

  4. Finite variance is the key condition. If \(\mathbb{V}[Y] = \infty\) (e.g., Cauchy distribution), the CLT fails and the \(\sqrt{n}\) rate no longer applies.

  5. Practical consequence. Even when errors are skewed, bimodal, or heteroskedastic, \(t\)-tests and confidence intervals based on the normal approximation are asymptotically valid — because the CLT operates on the sample mean, not on the raw errors.

(function() {
  function randNorm(mu, sigma) {
    let u = 0, v = 0;
    while(u === 0) u = Math.random();
    while(v === 0) v = Math.random();
    return mu + sigma * Math.sqrt(-2 * Math.log(u)) * Math.cos(2 * Math.PI * v);
  }

  const DISTS = {
    uniform:  { sample: () => Math.random(),                                      mu: 0.5,   sigma: Math.sqrt(1/12) },
    exp:      { sample: () => -Math.log(1 - Math.random()),                       mu: 1,     sigma: 1              },
    bimodal:  { sample: () => Math.random() < 0.5 ? randNorm(0.2,0.07) : randNorm(0.8,0.07), mu: 0.5, sigma: null },
    bernoulli:{ sample: () => Math.random() < 0.3 ? 1 : 0,                       mu: 0.3,   sigma: Math.sqrt(0.3*0.7) },
    triangle: { sample: () => 1 - Math.sqrt(1 - Math.random()),                  mu: 1/3,   sigma: null           }
  };

  function mean(a) { return a.reduce((s,x) => s+x, 0) / a.length; }
  function std(a)  { const m = mean(a); return Math.sqrt(a.reduce((s,x) => s+(x-m)**2, 0)/a.length); }
  function skew(a) { const m = mean(a), s = std(a); return s===0?0: a.reduce((t,x)=>t+((x-m)/s)**3,0)/a.length; }

  function histogram(data, bins) {
    const mn = Math.min(...data), mx = Math.max(...data), w = (mx-mn)/bins;
    const counts = Array(bins).fill(0);
    const labels = [];
    for(let i=0;i<bins;i++) labels.push((mn+i*w+w/2).toFixed(3));
    data.forEach(v => { let b=Math.floor((v-mn)/w); if(b>=bins)b=bins-1; counts[b]++; });
    return { labels, density: counts.map(c=>c/data.length/w) };
  }

    return Math.exp(-0.5*((x-mu)/sigma)**2)/(sigma*Math.sqrt(2*Math.PI));
  }

- The **Bernoulli** (p = 0.3) case is a good reminder that the CLT applies even to discrete distributions: for large $n$, the sampling distribution of a proportion is approximately normal.
:::

---

## Key takeaways {#sec-clt-takeaways}

1. **Centering and scaling matter.** The raw sample mean collapses to a point (LLN). Multiplying by $\sqrt{n}$ holds the variance fixed and reveals the distributional shape.

2. **Higher moments vanish at increasing rates.** Skewness shrinks at $n^{-1/2}$, kurtosis at $n^{-1}$, and so on. The $\sqrt{n}$-scaled sum retains only the variance.

3. **The normal is the attractor.** It is the unique fixed point of the averaging operator for distributions with finite variance, the maximum-entropy distribution given mean and variance, and the limit of any convolution sequence. It is not assumed — it emerges.

4. **Finite variance is the key condition.** If $\mathbb{V}[Y] = \infty$ (e.g., Cauchy distribution), the CLT fails and the $\sqrt{n}$ rate no longer applies.

5. **Practical consequence.** Even when errors are skewed, bimodal, or heteroskedastic, $t$-tests and confidence intervals based on the normal approximation are asymptotically valid — because the CLT operates on the *sample mean*, not on the raw errors.
